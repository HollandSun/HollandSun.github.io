Attention · Memory · Neuroimaging

How a learned readiness to shift attention shapes what we perceive and remember.

I am a Master's student in Psychology at Wake Forest University, advised by Dr. Anthony Sali. I study attentional control and its links to memory. One question is whether a learned attentional state, how ready we are to shift versus hold attention, becomes part of the context in which a memory is formed. Another is which stage of a spatial attention shift that learned flexibility actually changes. My second experiment on the first question found a benefit when the state matched, limited to items met early in learning. My thesis takes on the second.

Before Wake Forest, I spent three years at the Sleep and NeuroImaging Center at Southwest University in Chongqing, China (PI: Prof. Xu Lei), first as an undergraduate researcher and then as lab coordinator. There I used structural and functional MRI, machine learning and brain transcriptomics to identify biologically grounded subtypes of insomnia disorder, and ran data collection at four hospitals for a national insomnia project.

Two lines of work

Attention as an internal context

Wake Forest University · 2025–present · with Dr. Anthony Sali

People learn, without being told, how often a setting will ask them to move their attention (Sali, Anderson & Yantis, 2015). In mostly-shift blocks, shifting gets cheaper; in mostly-hold blocks, it stays costly. This shows up in the shift cost: how much slower people are when a cue tells them to move than when it tells them to stay. I ask what that learned flexibility is made of, and whether it is stored along with the things we encounter while in it.

Master's thesis · in progressJun 2026–present

The temporal dynamics of learned attentional flexibility: Investigating the role of disengagement

Committee: Dr. Anthony Sali, Dr. Janine Jennings, Dr. Stefon van Noordt

Learned flexibility shows up as a smaller shift cost, but a difference in response time cannot say which part of a shift changed, or when. A spatial shift has three parts: disengaging from the current location, moving, and engaging at the new one (Posner et al., 1984). My thesis tests whether the learned state works through the first.

  1. DisengageLower the priority of the location attention is on, so another location can take over. This is the step the thesis tests.
  2. MoveCarry attention toward the new location.
  3. EngageSettle attention on what is at the destination.

I treat disengagement in priority-map terms: it reduces the weight on the currently prioritized location. That way the account does not depend on attention sitting at only one place at a time.

Task
A compelled-response task, borrowed from studies of urgent saccadic choice (Shankar et al., 2011). A thick outline marks the starting location, carried over from the previous trial. When it disappears, the clock starts, and the response has to get under way before the participant knows what it should be. After 1,350–1,950 ms the starting circle turns blue or red, meaning shift or hold (counterbalanced), and a Landolt C appears in each circle. The participant reports whether the C at the to-be-attended location opens up or down, by 2,000 ms after the go signal. That leaves 50–650 ms after the cue. An eye tracker checks central fixation.
Design
Context × trial type × congruency, all within subject. Four mostly-shift blocks (70% shift) and four mostly-hold blocks (70% hold), 80 trials each: 640 trials in about 1.5 hours. On congruent trials the two Cs open the same way; on incongruent trials the C at the location to ignore points to the wrong answer. Planned N = 40, so that about 34 usable participants give 80% power for the key interaction.
Measure
Processing time is response time minus the gap: how long the cue had been on screen when the response came. Plotting accuracy against processing time gives a tachometric curve that climbs from chance to a ceiling. Its center point and rise time show when the cued C takes over the response, and how fast, separately for congruent and incongruent trials.
Prediction
If a mostly-shift context speeds disengagement, the C at the old location should stop interfering sooner on shift trials, so the congruency effect shrinks. On hold trials the same change should backfire: readiness to leave hands priority to the other location before the cue, so its C interferes more and the congruency effect grows. The key test is the context × trial type × congruency interaction on center point and rise time.

Attentional flexibility as an internal encoding context for explicit and implicit memory

Memory is better when the context at retrieval matches the context at encoding (Tulving & Thomson, 1973). This holds for external settings, such as learning on land or underwater, and for internal states, such as being sober or intoxicated. I ask whether a learned attentional state can serve as that context: is memory better when the shift-or-hold state at retrieval matches the state at encoding?

I designed both experiments, programmed them in MATLAB with Psychtoolbox, obtained IRB approval, ran the participants, and analyzed the data in R with (generalized) linear mixed-effects models and repeated-measures ANOVA.

Experiment 1 · Explicit memoryN = 21 · presented at NCCC, March 2026
Task
The letter-cue RSVP task at the top of this page. Four blocks of 60 trials, two mostly-shift and two mostly-hold. On 15 trials per block a word followed the attention trial. Words were studied in the first two blocks and tested with a two-choice recognition test in the last two.
Design
Mixed. Participants were tested either in the same block type they had studied in (congruent group) or in the other one (incongruent group).
Result
People learned the block regularity. In mostly-hold blocks, shift trials were about 200 ms slower than hold trials; in mostly-shift blocks the two did not differ. Recognition accuracy did not depend on whether the state matched.
Next
Few memory trials per condition survived screening, so a small effect could have been missed. Experiment 2 moved to a within-subject design and a continuous measure.
Experiment 2 · Implicit memoryN = 42 · to be presented at OPAM, November 2026
Task
The cue became a picture. Both streams showed phase-scrambled noise, and the cue was a degraded object image (20% image, 80% noise) in the attended stream: an animal meant shift, an inanimate object meant hold. Images came from the Konkle and Caramazza (2013) animacy-by-size set. Four blocks of 80 trials; mostly-shift blocks had 70% shift trials and mostly-hold blocks had 70% hold trials.
Design
Fully within-subject. The first two blocks, one of each type, were for encoding. 96 of their pictures came back as cues in the last two blocks, with the same meaning as before. Memory was measured implicitly, as response time on trials cued by a repeated picture.
S = mostly-shift block, H = mostly-hold block. A repeated picture keeps its cue meaning; only the block type around it can change.
Retrieval in SRetrieval in H
Encoding in SCongruentIncongruent
Encoding in HIncongruentCongruent
Result
The shift cost was again smaller in mostly-shift blocks (about 60 vs 140 ms at encoding), and the difference held at retrieval. Repeated pictures were handled faster when the block type matched. The benefit came from pictures first seen early in an encoding block, before performance leveled off, where it was about 45 ms. Pictures first seen late in a block showed none.
Next
Two accounts fit this pattern. The state may bind more strongly to items met while the task is still being learned. Or an abstract context cue may help more after a longer delay, and early items waited longer before they came back. In this design, learning stage and delay move together, so the next step is to pull them apart.

Heterogeneity in insomnia disorder

Southwest University · 2022–2025 · with Prof. Xu Lei

Case–control MRI studies of insomnia often disagree. One reason is that the diagnosis groups together people whose brains differ in different ways. With colleagues at the Sleep and NeuroImaging Center, I used semi-supervised machine learning to find data-driven subtypes, then tied them to sleep, cognition and regional gene expression. My part ranged from collecting data in hospitals to preprocessing, machine learning and imaging transcriptomics.

  1. 2022–2023

    Subtypes from gray matter volume

    HYDRA clustering of T1-weighted MRI in 56 patients and 73 controls found two reliable neuroanatomical subtypes. Biological Psychology, 2023

    My partPooled MRI from five earlier lab projects, preprocessed the T1w and DTI data in SPM and FSL, ran kernel SVM classification and HYDRA in MATLAB, and drafted parts of the Methods and Discussion.

  2. 2023–2024

    Response inhibition and frontal–striatal connectivity

    Young adults with insomnia stopped more slowly on the stop-signal task, and slower stopping went with stronger right superior frontal–putamen connectivity. J. Psychiatric Research, 2024

    My partScored an executive-function battery (stop-signal, digit span, task switching) for about 120 participants, ran CAT12 VBM and SPM12 resting-state preprocessing, helped with seed-based connectivity, and worked on the Methods and revision.

  3. 2023–2024

    Brain function, not structure, tracks gene expression

    Linked case–control imaging maps to Allen Human Brain Atlas transcriptomics, with replication in an independent sample. 677 genes correlated positively and 582 negatively with the main functional component; none tracked gray matter. PNP&BP, 2025

    My partComputed GMV, ALFF and ReHo maps and effect sizes, processed AHBA expression data across several atlases, ran the spatial permutation and bootstrap tests and the GO/KEGG, tissue and cell-type enrichment analyses, made the figures, and wrote parts of the Methods, Results and Discussion.

  4. 2024

    Subtypes from morphometric similarity networks

    Built individual cortical networks across 308 regions from five surface features and validated subtypes against wearable sleep EEG. Human Brain Mapping, 2025

    My partRan FreeSurfer, built the individual networks, mapped AHBA expression onto the same 308 regions (AHBA_SurfRecon), and extracted sleep latency, total sleep, NREM and REM time, REM cycles and wake time from the forehead EEG. Made the imaging and gene figures and helped answer reviewers on the methods.

  5. 2024–2025

    Subtypes with progression stages Project lead

    Applied SuStaIn to multimodal MRI (MIND networks, fALFF, ReHo, functional connectivity) to recover both subtypes and their disease stages, and mapped each trajectory to a sleep-EEG phenotype from portable forehead recordings.

    My partDesigned the study and did all of the preprocessing. Built MIND networks from T1w MRI and the resting-state measures used as SuStaIn input, and wrote my own processing for the raw forehead EEG. Written up as an internal lab report.

  1. 2025

    Gene expression is associated with brain function of insomnia disorder, rather than brain structure

    Zhang, H., Sun, H., Li, J., Lv, Z., Tian, Y., & Lei, X.

    Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology and Biological Psychiatry, 136, 111209

    Across a discovery sample (264 patients, 129 controls) and a validation sample (115, 160), insomnia-related changes in resting-state brain function were associated with regional gene expression, while gray matter changes were not. The genes pointed to HPA-axis regulation, ion transport and inhibitory neuron pathways.

    Zhang, H., Sun, H., Li, J., & Lei, X.
